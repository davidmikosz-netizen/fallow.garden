Natural History of Your Gut Biome

The Multitudes Within

Understanding what it means to be human has radically changed in the last fifteen years.

Similar to when we found out Earth was not the center of the universe and that we are part of nature's evolutionary story, we now understand that each human being is, in fact, a supraorganism. Every one of us contains more microbial cells than human ones.

This tool explores how we can take care of our interior critters and work together. When we get this wrong, our invisible inhabitants can hijack our reward systems, manipulate our cravings, and drive inflammation. When we get it right, we can lead happier, healthier, and more balanced lives.

This tool has three levels of "explanation depth" and it also contains a simulator to see how your daily choices can change how we work together.

2. The Mind-Gut Connection

How Gut & Brain Talk

Digestion isn't just a food chute—your gut lining acts like a sensitive sensor that tastes what comes through and sends constant messages up to your brain.

Taste Buds Down the Line

Your gut has chemical sensors much like taste buds. As foods like sauerkraut or buckwheat move along, your gut checks what is in them and lets your brain know about fullness and energy long before digestion finishes.

How Cravings Get Nudged

Messages travel along a main nerve cable called the vagus nerve. When fast-digesting sugar feeds certain gut microbes, they can nudge your brain's reward system—making you want more of the same sweet foods.

Chemical Messengers in Your Blood

A big share of the tiny chemical messengers floating in your blood are made by gut microbes. Feed helpful microbes varied plants and fiber, and they make calming compounds. Feed mostly sugar and ultra-processed foods, and pushier microbes can stir up soreness, sluggishness, or brain fog.
