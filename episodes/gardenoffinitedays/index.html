Actuarial Reference & FAQ

1. Why is the remaining day count surprisingly large?
Standard life expectancy at birth (~77–79 years) includes infant, adolescent, and early-adulthood hazards. Once you reach your 50s, you have already bypassed those statistical risks. Actuaries calculate conditional life expectancy: an average 56-year-old today has a baseline life expectancy of ~84–86 years (~11,000 days), with healthy cohorts reaching 88–92.
2. What about sudden accidental risks (car crashes, slips, freak events)?
In actuarial science (the Gompertz-Makeham law), mortality is divided into the biological aging curve and the Makeham constant—the baseline probability of pure external bad luck (car crashes, falls, acute poisoning). Cumulative lifetime accidental death odds total ~3–5% in standard populations. Because actuarial tables measure real populations, these accidental hazards are already baked into your baseline.
3. What are the highest-impact levers most calculators miss?
Epidemiological literature demonstrates that Cardiorespiratory Fitness ($VO_2\text{ Max}$) yields up to a 5-fold mortality risk reduction from lowest to highest quartile. Muscle Mass & Power buffers against fatal falls (a leading cascade of late-life decline). Cognitive Reserve & Complex Projects protect against post-retirement mental and physical shock.
4. What does "Compression of Morbidity" mean?
Coined by James Fries, compression of morbidity means squaring the life curve: living at near-100% cognitive and physical vitality throughout your 60s, 70s, and 80s, so that decline is compressed into a rapid, painless descent at the very end rather than decades of frailty.
