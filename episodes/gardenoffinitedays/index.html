Actuarial Reference & FAQ

1. Why is the remaining day count surprisingly large?
Standard life expectancy at birth (~77–79 years) includes infant, childhood, and early-adult hazards. Once you reach your 50s, you have already bypassed those statistical risks. Actuaries calculate conditional life expectancy: an average 56-year-old today has an actuarial baseline life expectancy of ~84–86 years (~11,000+ days), with healthy habits extending into the late 80s or 90s.
2. What about sudden accidental risks (car crashes, falls, freak events)?
In actuarial science (the Gompertz-Makeham law), mortality is divided into the biological aging curve and the Makeham constant—the baseline probability of pure external bad luck (car crashes, falls, acute poisoning). Cumulative lifetime accidental death odds total ~3–5% in standard populations. Because actuarial tables measure real populations, these accidental hazards are already baked into your baseline.
3. What are the highest-impact levers most calculators miss?
Epidemiological literature demonstrates that Cardiorespiratory Stamina yields up to a 5-fold mortality risk reduction from lowest to highest quartile. Muscle Strength buffers against fatal falls (a leading cascade of late-life decline). Deep Cognitive Projects protect against post-retirement mental and physical deceleration.
4. What does "Compression of Morbidity" mean?
Coined by Stanford physician James Fries, compression of morbidity means "squaring the life curve": maintaining near-100% physical vitality, mental clarity, and independent living throughout your 60s, 70s, and 80s, so that any eventual physical decline is compressed into a brief, gentle final chapter at the very end of life, rather than spending decades in chronic frailty.
